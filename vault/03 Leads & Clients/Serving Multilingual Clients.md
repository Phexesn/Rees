# Serving Multilingual Clients

**In short:** Many buyers prefer to work in their own language and on the apps they already use. You can serve them well by offering clear communication, using qualified interpreters and reliable translations, and being careful not to treat anyone differently because of national origin or language. **Treat cultural notes below as things some clients may mention, never as assumptions about any individual.**

## The population (what the data says)
- **Spanish** is by far the largest non-English language spoken at home in the US. A Center for Immigration Studies analysis of Census data reported about 41.5 million Spanish speakers, 3.5 million Chinese, 1.8 million Tagalog, 1.5 million Vietnamese and 1.1 million Korean speakers at home in 2018 ([CIS analysis](https://cis.org/Report/673-Million-United-States-Spoke-Foreign-Language-Home-2018); from search summary; CIS is an advocacy think tank using Census data, so check the Census tables directly: ACS table S1601 or C16001 on data.census.gov). Numbers are from 2018 and are outdated.
- **English proficiency** (self-reported): in older ACS releases, a smaller share of Vietnamese, Chinese and Korean home-language speakers reported speaking English "very well" than Spanish speakers (about 40% Vietnamese, about 45% Chinese and Korean, as reported in a Census release summary from 2014; stale). "Less than very well" is a self-rating, not a test.
- **Hispanic homeownership (NAHREP):** NAHREP's 2025 State of Hispanic Homeownership Report reports about 10.2 million Hispanic homeowner households, up from 9.8 million; a net gain of about 441,000 owner households in 2025; and a Hispanic homeownership rate of about 48.5% ([NAHREP release](https://nahrep.org/press-releases/2026/02/10/us-census-bureau-hispanics-post-net-gain-441000-homeowners-in-2025/); [NAHREP report page](https://nahrep.org/shhr/); from search summaries).
- **Asian American homeownership (AREAA):** AREAA's State of Asia America report series: one summary says the 2024 edition put the overall AAPI homeownership rate at 63.2% with Vietnamese Americans highest at 70%; the 2023-24 edition's regional rates ranged from 61.3% (West) to 69.4% (Midwest) ([Virginia Realtors summary](https://virginiarealtors.org/2024/05/09/three-key-takeaways-from-the-areaa-2023-24-state-of-asia-america-report/); [NAR Magazine](https://www.nar.realtor/magazine/real-estate-news/report-remarkable-surge-in-aapi-homeownership); from search summaries). The 2025 and 2026 editions exist but their figures were not retrieved. **Check the latest AREAA report** before quoting.
- **Share of buyers (NAR 2025 Profile):** one report says Hispanic/Latino buyers were 7% and Asian/Pacific Islander buyers 4% of recent buyers in NAR's 2025 survey, while Zillow's 2025 buyer report shows 14% and 8% (different surveys with different methods) ([660AM summary](https://660amtheanswer.com/national/5f536237-3664-435b-a028-bbb3c42424c0); [Zillow](https://www.zillow.com/research/buyers-housing-trends-report-2025-35688/amp)). NAR's data on buyers' primary language was not found.

## Fair Housing: the non-negotiables
- **Never steer** clients toward or away from areas based on language, national origin, race, or other protected class. Show homes that fit the client's stated criteria. See [[Fair Housing Advertising]].
- HUD's 2016 guidance explains that limited English proficiency (LEP) is not itself a protected class but is closely tied to national origin; using LEP as cover for intentional discrimination, or policies with an unjustified discriminatory effect, can violate the Fair Housing Act ([summary: NLIHC](https://nlihc.org/resource/hud-issues-limited-english-proficiency-fair-housing-guidance); from search summary). Check HUD's current guidance, as policies may change.
- **Don't assume.** Ask each client how they want to communicate and what they want in a home.
- **Don't refuse service** or offer worse service because a client speaks limited English.
- **Advertising in other languages:** the same fair-housing rules apply. Translated ads must say the same thing, accurately.
- **Don't advertise a "specialty"** in ways that exclude or target by national origin; it is fine to say "Se habla español" or list languages you speak.

## Interpreter vs. translation
- **Interpreter** = spoken, live. **Translation** = written.
- Best practice: use a **qualified interpreter or bilingual agent** for substantive discussions of contracts and money. Don't rely on a child or relative to interpret legal/financial terms, and don't use machine translation alone for contracts.
- NAR's guidance suggests using glossaries, forms and disclosures translated by a reliable source such as a federal or state agency, a REALTOR association, or an attorney, and says many local associations offer translated forms; NAR's Code of Ethics page notes that the English version is the only official version for enforcement ([NAR Magazine](https://www.nar.realtor/magazine/real-estate-news/law-and-ethics/relieve-confusion-for-clients-who-dont-speak-english); [NAR translations page](https://www.nar.realtor/about-nar/governing-documents/code-of-ethics-translations); from search summaries).
- In most states, the **contract in English controls**; translations are usually courtesy only. Ask your broker and attorney how your state treats translated documents. Offer a disclosure (e.g., "translation provided for convenience; English version governs"), approved by your broker.
- If you are bilingual, consider taking a recognized certification (for example, NAHREP or AREAA training; verify what's available). Don't claim fluency you don't have.
- Keep records of who interpreted and when.

## Apps people commonly use
Ask which channel the client prefers. Do not assume.
- **WhatsApp:** widely used among Spanish-speaking communities in many countries. Spanish-speaking US usage is anecdotal in what I found (unverified).
- **WeChat:** an agent-written source says Chinese buyers often respond faster on WeChat than on email, text or phone ([Inman, 2017](https://www.inman.com/2017/10/20/the-one-app-you-need-to-sell-to-chinese-buyers/); search result).
- **KakaoTalk:** common in Korean-speaking communities (unverified; no source found in this session).
- **Zalo:** common in Vietnamese-speaking communities (unverified; no source found in this session).
- **Compliance:** Business messaging on these apps still raises record-keeping, consent and brokerage-policy questions. Ask your broker before using a personal app for client communications. See [[Texting & Calling Leads (TCPA)]].

## Cultural considerations (published by community and industry sources)
Use as listening prompts. Individuals vary widely.
- **Chinese-speaking buyers:** industry portals note that numbers like 6, 8 and 9 are considered lucky and 4 is avoided; some buyers care about the front door and feng shui ([Juwai](https://list.juwai.com/chinese-buyer-tips/2023/11/understanding-chinese-superstitions-when-buying-a-house); [Inman, 2026](https://www.inman.com/2026/03/30/5-ways-feng-shui-may-show-up-in-buyer-conversations-in-2026/); search results). These sources are marketing/trade content and some are dated; the Inman piece itself cautions that not every Chinese buyer cares about feng shui. Respond if a client raises the topic; don't bring it up on assumption.
- **Hispanic/Latino buyers:** NAHREP reports that multigenerational households and family involvement in decisions are common in many families (the search summary mentions multigenerational financing; unverified detail). Ask who will be involved in decisions and include them in meetings.
- **Vietnamese and Korean-speaking buyers:** I did not find reliable community-published guidance in this session. Rather than guessing, ask clients about their preferences, and consult AREAA and local chapters.
- General: allow time; confirm understanding by asking clients to explain back ("teach-back"); provide written summaries.

## Practical steps
1. Ask at intake: "What language do you prefer for conversation? For documents?" ([[Template - Buyer Intake Form]]).
2. Arrange a qualified interpreter or a bilingual colleague (with your broker's OK).
3. Use translated glossaries from reliable sources.
4. Keep the same standards of service, disclosure, and timelines for everyone.
5. Follow your broker's policy on what goes in translated form.
