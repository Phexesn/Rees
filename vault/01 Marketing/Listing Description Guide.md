# Listing Description Guide

**In short:** A good listing description is specific, true, and easy to skim. Open with the one thing a buyer will remember, list the top features with real numbers, walk through the house in a logical order, add a few location facts, and stop. Keep phone numbers, links and showing details out of the public MLS remarks, because most MLSs ban them. Never describe anything you cannot back up, say when photos are staged or digitally altered, and describe the home rather than the kind of person who should buy it ([[Fair Housing Advertising]]).

> Verification note: official MLS rulebooks could not be opened while researching this note (the hosts were not reachable). MLS rules below come from search summaries of association and MLS help pages. Always check your own MLS's rules and input guide before you rely on a limit.

## 1. Structure that works

Use this order. Each part can be one or two sentences.

1. **Hook (first 100-150 characters).** Portals and MLS search results show only the start of the text, so put the most useful fact first. (Truncation length varies by site; treat "first line matters most" as common practice.)
2. **Top 3-5 features, with numbers.** Square footage, bedrooms/baths, lot size, year of the roof or HVAC, parking, garage size. Only include numbers you have confirmed from the tax record, MLS sheet, seller documents or your own measurement.
3. **Room flow.** Describe the house the way someone walks through it: entry, living space, kitchen, bedrooms, bathrooms, outdoor space. Name materials and appliances (quartz counters, gas range, hardwood floors) instead of adjectives.
4. **Location facts.** Distances and facts a buyer can check (for example "0.4 mile to the community park"). Avoid claims about who lives nearby or how "safe" or "exclusive" an area is (see the Fair Housing section below).
5. **Call to action, only where allowed.** In public remarks the call to action is usually just a final fact ("Seller will consider offers after the open house" is a showing/offer instruction and is also usually barred). Put "call me" language on your social posts, flyers and website, not in public remarks.

### Template (fill in your own verified facts)

> [Hook: biggest single selling point]. [Beds/baths/sq ft/lot]. [Kitchen: 2-3 specific features]. [Living areas: layout and one standout detail]. [Primary suite and other bedrooms]. [Outdoor space]. [Systems/updates with years]. [Two location facts a buyer can verify].

## 2. MLS public remarks: rules to follow

Rules differ by MLS. Patterns that show up repeatedly in the sources:

- **No contact information.** One Long Island-area association tells members not to put names, phone numbers, websites, emails or virtual-tour links in public remarks (from search summary of lirealtor.com). A separate local MLS rule excerpt also bars showing instructions, offer procedures, property addresses, open houses and references to broker compensation, and says violations can lead to the field being deleted and a fine (from search summary, MLS not identified in the summary).
- **No open house details in remarks.** NorthstarMLS and CRMLS guidance, as summarised in an earlier research draft, says open house times belong in the dedicated open-house fields (search-snippet level, not read in full).
- **Agent contact goes in the designated field.** CRMLS directs agent phone/email/web to a separate Syndication Remarks field (search-snippet level).
- **Private remarks are for agents only.** The RESO data dictionary says private remarks must be hidden from the public (https://ddwiki.reso.org/display/DDW20/PrivateRemarks+Field, search-snippet level). Never paste private-remark content into public text.

### Character limits: verified examples (and how shaky they are)

There is no national limit. Examples seen in search results:

| Source | Figure | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Long Island-area association page (lirealtor.com) | Public remarks max 1,960 characters | From search summary of the association's help page |
| A "gtar.zendesk.com" help page titled "Maximum Characters Allowed" | Public Remarks: 2,000 characters | From search summary; the summary did not say which MLS the page covers |
| A Wisconsin-area association (wmlar.com) | Public Remarks - Expanded raised to 1,000 characters in Aug 2025 | From search summary |
| MLSPIN "Firm Remarks" | Expanded to 1,000 characters (this is a firm-remarks field, not public remarks) | From search summary |
| Vendor blogs | Range from about 1,000 to 4,000 for different MLSs; Bright MLS and MRED figures conflict between pages | Unverified, vendor content |

**Practical rule:** type your draft into your MLS's input form and read its live character counter. If you plan to reuse the text on several MLSs or portals, write a tight version under about 1,000 characters first and expand only if your MLS allows it (1,000 is a conservative working number, not a rule).

## 3. Words that sell vs. tired clichés

Sources agree that vague praise adds nothing and that concrete detail does the work.

**Replace these:**

| Tired | Better |
|---|---|
| nestled | name the street type or setting: "on a dead-end street", "backs to a greenbelt" |
| must see / won't last / priced to sell | delete; give the fact that makes it a good value |
| charming, cozy, dream home | what makes it so: "original 1940s oak floors", "built-in breakfast nook" |
| stunning, gorgeous, amazing | the feature: "10-foot ceilings", "south-facing windows" |
| TLC, potential, opportunity, bargain, cosmetic | say what needs work, factually: "kitchen is original to 1985" |

Inman, EliteAgent and Zillow all call out phrases like "must see" and "won't last" as filler (see Sources). Zillow's data summary, as reported in a search summary, says listings using "potential" sold for about 4.3% less than expected and "opportunity" about 2% less; those percentages are second-hand and I could not read Zillow's own page, so treat them as "reported", not proven.

**Words that tend to work** are concrete and checkable: material names (quartz, subway tile, hardwood), named appliances, "new roof (2023)", "fenced yard", "primary suite on main level". Zillow publishes a list of words associated with higher sale prices (https://www.zillow.com/blog/15-words-that-add-value-171182/); I could only see the title and URL, not the content, so check it yourself.

## 4. Honesty rules

- Describe only what is true and that you have confirmed. If you did not measure it or see the document, either verify it or leave it out.
- No exaggeration. "Brand new kitchen" is false if it was updated in 2009.
- Do not hide material defects by omission in a way that misleads. Disclosure duties vary by state; ask your broker.
- **Disclose staging and edited images.** NAR's Code of Ethics Article 12 requires a "true picture" in advertising, and a vendor summary of NAR guidance says altered images should be clearly labelled (vendor sources, so verify with NAR). California AB 723 requires disclosure on digitally altered listing images and access to unaltered originals from 1 January 2026 (reported by a vendor page and described in a California Senate committee analysis, https://sjud.senate.ca.gov/media/1938). See [[Phone Photo Editing (Free Apps)]].
- Add a plain line such as "Photos 3, 5 and 8 are virtually staged." in the field your MLS provides for it, or in the remarks if the MLS has no field.
- Do not use AI text unchecked. Read it aloud, delete anything you cannot verify. See [[AI in Real Estate - Rules & Risks]].

## 5. Fair Housing: describe the home, not the buyer

HUD advertising rules (24 C.F.R. Part 109) apply to listing text. Practical guidance from association and fair-housing-institute word lists:

- Avoid wording that targets or excludes a group: references to children or families as a preference, "adult community" unless it is legally restricted housing, religion ("near church" as a selling point), "exclusive" or "safe" neighborhoods, "perfect for a single woman".
- Standard feature terms such as "family room" or "walk-in closet" are generally fine when the ad shows no preference (from a search summary of fair housing guidance).
- A "we comply with fair housing" statement does not fix discriminatory wording.
- Neutral replacements: "quiet street", "fenced yard", "three bedrooms plus office", "0.3 mile to the library".

Read the dedicated note: [[Fair Housing Advertising]].

## 6. Three before/after rewrites

These are invented examples for practice. The facts in brackets are placeholders; use your own verified facts.

**Example 1: cliché pile-up**

- Before: *"Nestled in a charming neighborhood, this must-see dream home won't last long! Priced to sell!"*
- After: *"Three-bedroom brick ranch on a quiet dead-end street, 1,450 sq ft on a 0.22-acre lot. Updated kitchen with quartz counters and gas range. New roof [year]. Two-car garage."*
- Why: every claim is a fact a buyer can check.

**Example 2: steering language**

- Before: *"Perfect for a young family! Safe neighborhood, great for kids, close to church."*
- After: *"Fenced backyard, three bedrooms plus a bonus room, and a walk-in pantry. [X] minutes' walk to [named park]. Assigned school information available from the district."*
- Why: it describes features and lets the buyer decide who the home suits. (Also see [[Fair Housing Advertising]].)

**Example 3: exaggeration**

- Before: *"Stunning luxury chef's kitchen with top-of-the-line everything!"*
- After: *"Kitchen with 36-inch gas range, stainless hood, shaker cabinets and a 7-foot island with seating for three. Appliances stay."*
- Why: "luxury" and "top-of-the-line" are opinions; the specifics are not.

## 7. Final checklist

- [ ] Hook states the single best fact.
- [ ] Every number matches the MLS sheet, tax record or a document.
- [ ] No phone, email, link, showing instruction, open house time, or commission mention.
- [ ] Under my MLS's live character limit.
- [ ] No Fair Housing risk words; I described the home, not the buyer.
- [ ] Staged/edited photos are disclosed.
- [ ] I read it aloud and cut every cliché.
